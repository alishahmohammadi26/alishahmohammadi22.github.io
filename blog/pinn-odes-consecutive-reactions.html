01, The Problem

Reaction Rate Constants from 14 Concentration Measurements

The sequence $A \to B \to C$ is the workhorse template for pharmaceutical chemistry. In API synthesis, the intermediate $B$ may be the target compound, you want to quench the reaction at peak $[B]$. In drug stability studies, $B$ is often a toxic impurity. In metabolic modelling, $B$ is an active metabolite.

In every case, you need $k_1$ and $k_2$ to design the process. But experiments are expensive, a typical stability protocol yields 8–14 concentration measurements per species across the time course. The inverse problem: recover both rate constants from these sparse, noisy observations.

$$\frac{dC_A}{dt} = -k_1 C_A, \qquad \frac{dC_B}{dt} = k_1 C_A, k_2 C_B, \qquad \frac{dC_C}{dt} = k_2 C_B$$

The intermediate peak occurs at $t^* = \ln(k_1/k_2)/(k_1-k_2)$, its location encodes the ratio $k_1/k_2$ with high sensitivity.

Fig. A — PINN solution for the three-state A→B→C consecutive reaction. The critical challenge is accurately tracking the transient intermediate B, which peaks and then depletes. The residual-based network captures this without any labeled data.

02, The Paper

Ji et al. (2021) Stiff-PINN, A→B→C as the Benchmark

Ji, W., Qiu, W., Shi, Z., Pan, S. & Deng, S. (2021). Stiff-PINN: Physics-Informed Neural Network for Stiff Chemical Kinetics. J. Phys. Chem. A 125(36):8098–8106.

Ji et al. used A→B→C as their non-stiff validation benchmark before tackling stiff systems. Their central finding: PINNs recover rate constants jointly with the smooth trajectory in a single training run, no need for a least-squares optimiser wrapped around an ODE integrator. This notebook is the direct implementation of that benchmark.

Also referenced: SPIN-ODE (2025), sparse physics-informed networks for ODE parameter estimation in reaction systems, which extends this approach to higher-order and reversible reactions.

The critical advantage of the multi-output network: $\mathcal{N}(t) \to (C_A, C_B, C_C)$ learns all three species simultaneously. The physics loss implicitly enforces mass conservation $C_A + C_B + C_C = A_0$ throughout the domain, even between sparse measurement points. A data-only regression cannot provide this constraint.

04, Implementation & Results

k₁ = 1.003, k₂ = 0.598, Under 0.5% Error from 14 Points

Python · PyTorch
# Multi-output network: t → (CA, CB, CC)
# Learnable rate constants via softplus (always positive)
raw_k1 = nn.Parameter(torch.tensor(0.0, device=device))
raw_k2 = nn.Parameter(torch.tensor(0.0, device=device))

def loss_fn():
    C = model(t_col)
    Ca, Cb, Cc = C[:,0:1], C[:,1:2], C[:,2:3]
    k1, k2 = sp(raw_k1), sp(raw_k2)
    rA = d_dt(Ca, t_col) + k1*Ca           # ≡ 0 at exact solution
    rB = d_dt(Cb, t_col) - (k1*Ca - k2*Cb)
    rC = d_dt(Cc, t_col) - k2*Cb
    l_phys = torch.mean(rA**2 + rB**2 + rC**2)
    l_data = torch.mean((model(t_data) - c_data)**2)
    l_ic   = torch.mean((model(t0) - c0_true)**2)
    return l_data + 0.5*l_phys + l_ic, l_data, l_phys
Actual notebook output14 noisy concentration measurements, 2% Gaussian noise, Adam (12,000 epochs) + L-BFGS (500 steps). Recovered: k₁ = 1.003 (true 1.0, error 0.3%), k₂ = 0.598 (true 0.6, error 0.3%). Mass conservation CA+CB+CC automatically satisfied to <0.1% throughout the domain, no explicit constraint added.

Why does this work so well? Three independent observables constrain the two unknowns: $C_A$'s decay rate directly constrains $k_1$; $C_B$'s peak time constrains $k_1/k_2$; mass conservation provides a redundant check. Even if you remove $C_B$ from the data loss, the physics residual reconstructs it from $C_A$ and $C_C$ alone.
