Physics-Informed Neural Networks: Why Embedding Physical Laws Changes Everything

How adding a differential equation to the loss function lets a network learn the right answer from almost no data — and where that trick stops working.

PINN Foundations Beginner ·Sep 2025 ·18 min read

01 The problem with data-hungry models

When there isn't enough data

A standard neural network is an interpolator: give it thousands of labeled examples and it will approximate the function that produced them. In engineering and the physical sciences that assumption quietly fails. Experiments are expensive, sensors are sparse, and the region you most want to predict is often the one you have no measurements for. A purely data-driven model asked to extrapolate there will produce something smooth, confident, and wrong.

But we usually know something the data doesn't contain directly: the governing equation. Conservation of mass, a reaction rate law, a diffusion equation. The central idea of a physics-informed neural network is to stop treating that equation as background knowledge and start treating it as part of the loss.

02 The core idea

Physics as a loss function

We train the network to minimize two things at once: how far its predictions sit from any data we do have, and how far its own derivatives sit from satisfying the differential equation. The total objective is a weighted sum:

$$ \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \underbrace{\frac{1}{N_d}\sum_{i=1}^{N_d}\big(\hat{y}(x_i;\theta) - y_i\big)^2}_{\text{data}} \;+\; \lambda\,\underbrace{\frac{1}{N_r}\sum_{j=1}^{N_r} r\big(x_j;\theta\big)^2}_{\text{physics residual}} $$

where the residual for a first-order ODE \( \frac{dy}{dx} = f(x,y) \) is simply \( r(x;\theta) = \frac{d\hat{y}}{dx} - f(x,\hat{y}) \), and that derivative comes from automatic differentiation of the network itself — not a finite-difference stencil.

The core insight

The physics term is evaluated at collocation points where you have no label at all. That's why a PINN can learn from little or no measured data: the equation supplies the supervision everywhere the sensors don't.

03 In code

A first PINN, end to end

Here is the residual and training step for a minimal PINN in PyTorch. Note that the derivative is obtained with torch.autograd.grad — the same engine that computes weight gradients also gives us dy/dx.

train_pinn.pyPython · PyTorch
def residual(model, x):
    x.requires_grad_(True)
    y = model(x)
    dy_dx = torch.autograd.grad(y, x,
        grad_outputs=torch.ones_like(y),
        create_graph=True)[0]
    # ODE: dy/dx = -k*y  ->  r = dy/dx + k*y
    return dy_dx + K * y

def loss_fn(model, x_data, y_data, x_colloc):
    l_data = ((model(x_data) - y_data) ** 2).mean()
    l_phys = (residual(model, x_colloc) ** 2).mean()
    return l_data + LAMBDA * l_phys

Training is the familiar Adam loop, and — as in every post in this series — we finish with a short L-BFGS polish once the loss has flattened.

04 Trade-offs

Where PINNs win, and where they don't

PINNs are not a replacement for a good mesh solver on a well-posed forward problem. Their advantage shows up in three places: sparse or noisy data, inverse problems where parameters are unknown, and smooth high-dimensional domains where meshing is painful.

Honest limitations

Vanilla PINNs struggle with stiff systems, sharp fronts, and high-frequency solutions — the spectral-bias wall. Later posts fix these with time-scaling, adaptive sampling, and hard-constraint ansätze; this first one is deliberately the easy case.
